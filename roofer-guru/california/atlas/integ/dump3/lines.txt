Service Lines

A California roofing company is thirteen businesses that share a crew yard. Replacements come due as roofs age, in waves set by the decades the tracts were built; leaks and wind damage arrive with the wet season, November to March; wildfire work comes in rebuilds that run for years; fire hardened roofs are bought on an insurer's deadline; tile roofs need new underlayment while the tile itself goes back; a reroof under solar panels starts with taking them off; metal, manufactured home, multifamily, commercial, coating and new construction work each has its own buyer and season. Each line here has its own heatmap, statewide volume, jobs open to marketing, gross profit pool, season, rules, keywords, competitors and bid modifiers, with every assumption in plain view and editable.

READ THIS FIRST: WHAT A SERVICE LINE IS HERE, AND WHAT THE NUMBERS ARE
A line counts roofing jobs a year at each ZIP (the volume), the share an owner gives to a contractor found through search, ads, referrals or the door (jobs open to marketing), and those jobs times a default ticket times a 33% gross margin (the gross profit pool). Replacement and tile start from the roof cohort model; repair from the American Housing Survey leak rate; storm from a state total (the survey's share of roof jobs paid by insurance) spread by each ZIP's roofs and its wind and atmospheric river exposure; fire from CAL FIRE's damage inspections; hardening from the homes insurers rate High or Extreme for wildfire and the FAIR Plan's policies; solar from the reroofs that sit under panels; new construction from Census building permits; metal, manufactured home, multifamily, commercial and coating lines from stated assumptions. Every default below is a placeholder until it is replaced with your own job history: change it and every module updates. Nothing here is a bid, a quote, a forecast for one company, or legal advice.

The reroof of a house, duplex or small building at the end of the covering's life. California single family roofs are 62 percent composition shingles, 27 percent tile (18 clay, 9 concrete) and 5 percent wood by the 2020 federal Residential Energy Consumption Survey (837 California homes; the occupant names the material). A replacement removes every layer down to the deck (Residential Code R908.3). Replacing more than 50 percent of the roof triggers the Energy Code cool roof rule in most inland and southern climate zones, and in a fire zone replacing 50 percent or more in 12 months triggers the Class A new roof rules. The City of Los Angeles issued 14,511 reroof permits in 2023 after the wet winter, against 10,383 in 2022, and 11,954 in 2025; the median house roof there is 22 squares. The line in law

What California and federal rules do to this line's ads, contracts and jobs. Each item carries its citation; module 03 has the full register.

A contractor license for any job of $1,000 or more: roofing is class C-39
A
Bus. & Prof. Code 7026, 7048 (Stats. 2024, ch. 240, AB 2622; Stats. 2025, ch. 67, AB 1170); Cal. Code Regs. tit. 16, 832.39 · $1,000 line from January 1, 2025 (it was $500)

Anyone who offers, bids or does work to build, alter, repair or improve a structure is a contractor, subcontractors included. The only exemption is minor work: the total price for labor, materials and everything else is under $1,000, and the work needs no building permit, and the person employs no one. Splitting a job into smaller contracts to stay under the line is not allowed. The roofing class is C-39: it covers installing and repairing surfaces that seal, waterproof and weatherproof structures, from shingles, tile, slate and shakes to metal roofing systems, urethane foam and membranes. In practice almost every reroof needs a licensed contractor, because the price is over $1,000 and most cities require a permit (that last sentence is our reading, grade D). What a roofer must do: hold an active C-39 (or another class that covers the work) before offering or bidding any roofing job.

The license number goes in every ad, contract and bid
B
Bus. & Prof. Code 7030.5; 7027.1 and 7027.2 (Stats. 2024, ch. 240, AB 2622) · In force; 7027.2 as amended January 1, 2025

Every licensee must include the license number in all construction contracts, in subcontracts and calls for bid, and in all forms of advertising, as the registrar prescribes. Advertising as a contractor without a license is a misdemeanor with a fine of $700 to $1,000 on top of any other punishment. An unlicensed person may advertise only for jobs under $1,000 and only if the ad says the person is not licensed. What a roofer must do: print the CSLB license number on every ad, page, truck, sign, door hanger, estimate and contract. Section 7030.5 was read on a transcription host, so it is graded B; sections 7027.1 and 7027.2 were read as archive captures of the Legislature's pages.

A written home improvement contract for any residential job over $500
A
Bus. & Prof. Code 7151, 7159 (Stats. 2020, ch. 312, SB 1474; 2026 text with AB 1327) · In force

Home improvement means repairing, remodeling, altering or modernizing residential property, and rebuilding a home damaged or destroyed by a disaster with a proclaimed state of emergency. Reroofing is home improvement. When the total price is over $500 the contract must be in writing, signed and dated by both sides, and a copy given to the buyer before any work starts. It must carry the contractor's name, business address and license number; the salesperson's name and registration number; the heading "Home Improvement" in at least 10 point bold; the contract price; any finance charge shown separately; a description of the project and the significant materials; the down payment; a schedule of progress payments; approximate start and completion dates; and a note on extra work and change orders. Required notices cover commercial general liability insurance, workers' compensation, the Mechanics Lien Warning, information about the CSLB, the right to cancel, and the right to require a performance and payment bond. Text is at least 10 point, the main warnings 12 point bold. A change order is in writing and signed before the changed work starts.

Down payment: $1,000 or 10 percent, whichever is less
A
Bus. & Prof. Code 7159.5 (Stats. 2023, ch. 403, SB 601); 7159(d)(8)(C), (d)(9)(C) · In force

A down payment on a home improvement contract may not exceed $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever amount is less, and the contract must print that sentence. Apart from the down payment a contractor may neither ask for nor accept payment that exceeds the value of the work performed or the material delivered. Breaking the price, down payment or advance payment rules is a misdemeanor: a fine of $100 to $5,000, up to one year in county jail, or both; in an area damaged by a disaster with a proclaimed emergency the court must impose the maximum fine. On request the contractor gives an unconditional lien release for the part already paid, and the owner may hold further payments until it comes. A contractor who furnishes a performance and payment bond, or a blanket bond or joint control the registrar has approved, is exempt from the down payment and progress payment limits.

Right to cancel: three business days, five for a senior, seven after a disaster
A
Bus. & Prof. Code 7159(e)(6), (e)(7), (f); Civil Code 1689.6 · Five day rule for contracts from January 1, 2021

The buyer may cancel a home improvement contract until midnight of the third business day after receiving a signed and dated copy that includes the notice. For a buyer who is a senior citizen the notice says five and fifth. A contract written to repair or restore a home damaged by a sudden or catastrophic event with a federal, state or local emergency declaration carries a notice titled "Seven-Day Right to Cancel". The buyer cancels by emailing, mailing, faxing or delivering a written notice to the contractor's place of business, and the contractor returns anything paid within 10 days of receiving it. The notice is not required for a contract negotiated at the contractor's place of business. The age that makes a buyer a senior citizen for this rule was not read in the sections opened, and neither was the Civil Code's definition of a business day: confirm both before relying on a date.

From 2026: cancellation by email, and subcontractors named on request
A
Bus. & Prof. Code 7159 (AB 1327, Aguiar-Curry); SB 517 (Niello); CSLB Industry Bulletin 25-04 · January 1, 2026

The first page of a home improvement contract must now carry the name, address and email address of the contractor to whom the Notice of Cancellation is sent, and a telephone number that helps the buyer find and fill in the notice. Buyers may cancel by email. A contractor who uses subcontractors on a home improvement project must, on request, give each subcontractor's name, contact details, license number and classification. What a roofer must do: update every contract form and watch the cancellation inbox every day.

Reroof permits depend on the city
A
City of Los Angeles permit data; San Diego Information Bulletin 123; Los Angeles County Building Code Manual 1512 Article 1; San Francisco permit data; Labor Code 3800 · As read November 19, 2026

There is no single state reroof permit statute in the sources read; each city or county decides. City of Los Angeles: permits are issued for reroofs in very large numbers on a standard description (11,954 in 2025); the municipal code sentence that requires them was not opened. City of San Diego: no building permit to renew a roof covering where the roof structure is not altered; a permit is needed if framing or sheathing is altered, if shakes or shingles give way to material heavier than 6 pounds per square foot, if skip sheathing is replaced, or on a historic property, and up to 25 percent of the deck may be replaced in kind without one on houses, duplexes and townhouses. Los Angeles County (unincorporated): a building permit for the reroof, plus a separate solar permit if panels come off and go back. San Francisco: reroof permits are filed over the counter (4,084 in 2025). San Jose and Sacramento were not opened. Permit counts therefore measure the reroof market only where a permit is required.

Tear off, recover and relaying tile
B
California Residential Code R908.3, R908.3.1.1, R908.4, R908.5 (2022 and 2025); California Building Code 1505.1.2 (2022) · 2025 edition in force January 1, 2026

A roof replacement removes every existing layer down to the deck. A second layer is not allowed where the roof is water soaked or deteriorated, where the existing covering is slate, clay, cement or asbestos cement tile, or where there are already two or more layers. Sound slate, clay or cement tile may be reinstalled; damaged, cracked or broken tile may not, and rusted or damaged flashings, edgings and vents are replaced. That sentence is the legal basis for a tile lift and relay. Tile or metal over wood shakes needs a fire barrier layer first. The 2025 code lets existing self adhered underlayment stay under conditions. Outside fire zones the statewide floor is Class C, for new roofs, for the whole roof when more than 50 percent is replaced in a year, and for any repaired part. Code text was read on UpCodes, not the official viewer.

Class A roof in a fire zone when 50 percent or more is replaced in 12 months
B
2025 California Wildland-Urban Interface Code (Title 24, Part 7) 101.3.1, 504.2, 507.1; 2022 California Building Code 705A.1, 1505.1.1 · January 1, 2026 (the 2022 code said "more than 50 percent")

The 2025 edition moved the wildfire construction rules from Chapter 7A of the Building Code into a new Wildland-Urban Interface Code. It applies on all State Responsibility Area land, in High and Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zones in Local Responsibility Areas, and in local interface areas. On a building that existed before the code, a roof covering that is replaced, or has 50 percent or more replaced in a 12 month period, must be replaced with the covering required for new construction, and every part applied in an addition, alteration or repair must be at least Class A. The roof must be a Class A assembly when tested to ASTM E108 or UL 790. Class A is the rating of an assembly (covering, underlayment and deck together), not of a shingle or a tile on its own. Some assemblies count as Class A without a test, such as clay or concrete tile, metal or slate on a noncombustible deck. Under the 2022 code the trigger was more than 50 percent within one year, in any Fire Hazard Severity Zone. A jurisdiction that adopts the State Fire Marshal's model ordinance is outside the state sentence (2022 text).

Cool roof rules when more than 50 percent of a roof is replaced
A
Title 24, Part 6 (2025 Energy Code) 110.8(i), 150.2(b)1I, 180.2(b)1A, 141.0(b)2B, 140.3(a)1A; the 2022 code has the same values · 2025 code for permit applications from January 1, 2026

The Energy Code has 16 climate zones, set by ZIP code. A low sloped roof has a rise to run under 2:12. Single family: when more than 50 percent of the roof is replaced, a new layer over the old one included, steep roofs in zones 4 and 8 to 15 need an aged solar reflectance of at least 0.20 and thermal emittance of 0.75, or a Solar Reflectance Index of 16; low sloped roofs in zones 4 and 6 to 15 need 0.63 and 0.75, or an index of 75. Multifamily: the trigger is more than 50 percent of the roof area or more than 2,000 square feet, whichever is less, replaced, recovered or recoated; low slope in zones 2, 4 and 6 to 15 needs 0.63 (index 75); steep in zones 4 and 8 to 15 needs 0.20 (index 16). Nonresidential, same trigger: low slope in every zone needs 0.63 (index 75); steep needs 0.20 (index 16) in zones 1 and 3 and 0.25 with emittance 0.80 (index 23) in zones 2 and 4 to 16. Hotel and motel guest rooms: low slope 0.55 (index 64) in zones 9, 10, 11, 13, 14 and 15; steep 0.20 in zones 2 to 15. Products are rated and labeled by the Cool Roof Rating Council; an unrated asphalt shingle is assumed to have an aged reflectance of 0.08 and any other unrated product 0.10.

Cool roof exceptions, and the insulation a low slope reroof also triggers
A
Title 24, Part 6 (2025 Energy Code) exceptions to 150.2(b)1Ii and 150.2(b)1Iiia; Tables 150.2-E, 150.2-F, 141.0-B, 141.0-C; 180.2(b)1Aiii · 2025 code for permit applications from January 1, 2026

Single family steep roofs are exempt where the attic is insulated to R-38 (or the ceiling U-factor is 0.025 or lower), where there is a radiant barrier in the attic not laid directly above spaced sheathing, where there are no ducts in the attic in zones 2, 4, 9, 10, 12 and 14, or where R-2 or more continuous insulation sits above or below the deck. Roof constructions weighing at least 25 pounds per square foot are exempt in every building type; that is why most concrete and clay tile reroofs are exempt (the tile inference is ours, grade D). Roof area under building integrated solar is exempt. Single family low slope roofs may trade reflectance for deck insulation, down to no reflectance requirement at R-10 in zones 6 and 7 or R-24 in zones 2, 4 and 8 to 15. A low slope reroof also triggers insulation: R-14 continuous or U-0.039 for single family and multifamily in zones 1, 2, 4 and 8 to 16 (a multifamily recoat is exempt); for nonresidential, R-23 in zones 1 to 5 and 9 to 16 and R-17 in zones 6 to 8, with at least R-10 above the deck, and a recoat is exempt. The nonresidential insulation table was read in the 2025 code only.

Wood shakes: listed products only statewide, banned outright in Los Angeles
A
Health & Safety Code 13132.7(b), (d), (f), (j); Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety Information Bulletin P/BC 2026-013; Los Angeles Ordinance 165047; San Diego Information Bulletin 123 · Los Angeles since August 25, 1989; bulletin effective January 1, 2026

No wood roof covering may be sold or applied in California unless the State Fire Marshal has approved and listed it and it has passed at least 5 years of the 10 year natural weathering test. The statute's floor is Class A in Very High zones, Class B in State Responsibility Areas other than Moderate, and Class C everywhere else; the building codes now go further in fire zones. Cities may be stricter. In the City of Los Angeles all wood shake and wood shingle roof covering is prohibited for new work, reroofing and repairs; only a minor repair up to 5 percent may be allowed on request, and reroofing more than 50 percent in a year means all remaining shakes come off. The City of San Diego permits no wood shingle or shake renewals and no new covering over existing wood. A 2003 Orange County grand jury report lists other bans (Santa Barbara County, the City of Santa Barbara, Carlsbad, Del Mar, El Cajon, Vista and others); a current statewide list was not found. About 5.4 percent of California single family homes still report wood roofs (2020 federal survey).

Roof warranties: in writing, transferable, and "lifetime" must say whose
B
Civil Code 1797.91, 1797.92, 1797.93, 1797.94 · Contracts from January 1, 1994

A contract for roofing materials, or to install, repair or replace all or part of the roof of a residential structure, must be in writing if it includes any warranty that runs past completion. The warranty passes to every later buyer of the home unless a clear and conspicuous clause limits transfer; that limit must sit in a box at the top of the first page of the warranty in 14 point bold. A warranty that uses "lifetime", "life" or a similar word must disclose, clearly enough to be noticed and understood, the life it refers to.

The installer certifies the roof's fire class to the owner
B
Health & Safety Code 13132.7(i) · In force

The installer of a roof covering must give the building owner the certification of the roof covering classification, as the manufacturer or supplier provides it, and give it to the building department on request. The installer must also install the covering in accordance with the manufacturer's listing. What a roofer must do: hand over the classification sheet with the final invoice and keep a copy in the job file.

A financing ad that states a payment amount, a number or period of payments, a down payment or a finance charge must also state the down payment, the terms of repayment and the annual percentage rate, using that term. Truthful advertising: what was read and what was not
D
Penal Code 396(h); Civil Code 1797.93; Cal. Code Regs. tit. 10, 2644.9; FTC Act section 5 · In force

California's general unfair competition and false advertising statutes were not read for this build, so the register cites no section for them. What was read: a price gouging violation is also unfair competition (Penal Code 396); a "lifetime" roof warranty must say whose or what lifetime (Civil Code 1797.93); the Safer from Wildfires regulation sets no discount percentage; and Class A is the rating of a tested assembly. On the fire evidence, about 90 percent of structures inside the January 2025 Palisades and Eaton perimeters had Class A coverings by CAL FIRE's inspection data, and IBHS found those roofs were often paired with vulnerable vents, eaves, windows and doors. Copy must therefore never say "fireproof", promise a discount size or promise that an insurer will renew. Superlatives need a dated, verifiable basis.

Leaks and repairs follow the rain and peak in February; wind and rain damage follows the atmospheric rivers and winter fronts with a lag, busiest in January and February (2.2 times an average month). How the lines are built

Anchor, apportionment and grade for each line.

Roof replacement B	The reroof of a house, duplex or small building at the end of the covering's life. California single family roofs are 62 percent composition shingles, 27 percent tile (18 clay, 9 concrete) and 5 percent wood by the 2020 federal Residential Energy Consumption Survey (837 California homes; the occupant names the material). A replacement removes every layer down to the deck (Residential Code R908.3). Replacing more than 50 percent of the roof triggers the Energy Code cool roof rule in most inland and southern climate zones, and in a fire zone replacing 50 percent or more in 12 months triggers the Class A new roof rules. The City of Los Angeles issued 14,511 reroof permits in 2023 after the wet winter, against 10,383 in 2022, and 11,954 in 2025; the median house roof there is 22 squares. No California field study of shingle life was found, so the wear out age in the model is an assumption set in the data build.
Roof repair and leaks B	Leak tracing, flashing, vent, skylight and valley repairs, slipped or broken tile, and small sections of covering. Any repaired part must meet the fire class for its location: at least Class C statewide and Class A in a fire zone. Leak calls follow rain: the wet winter of 2022 to 2023 was followed by a 40 percent rise in Los Angeles reroof permits. A repair of $750 or less that the owner asked for may use the short service and repair contract; above that the full home improvement contract applies. Since July 1, 2025 a crew repairing a leak needs fall protection wherever the fall is 6 feet or more. The share of leaks fixed by a paid roofer is an assumption set in the data build.
Wind and rain damage C	Damage from winter storm wind and rain and from dry offshore wind. NOAA Storm Events lists 7,173 high wind events in California from 1996 to 2026. In forecasters' narratives roof damage appears with gusts of about 60 to 70 mph and above (our reading; the database gives no threshold). The largest Santa Ana roof damage event in the record is October 21, 2007, with gusts over 70 mph and $35 million of reported damage in the Inland Empire alone; on January 4, 2008 a winter storm brought 71 mph near Elk Grove. Rain does its damage through leaks: from December 2022 to March 2023 the database lists 816 flood events. Storm Events dollar figures are forecasters' estimates and are known to be incomplete. A home insured only by the FAIR Plan needs a separate Difference in Conditions policy for wind or water. The damage rates in the model are assumptions set in the data build.
Wildfire damage and rebuild roofing C	Roofing for homes inside fire perimeters. The January 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires destroyed 16,264 structures and damaged 2,051 (CAL FIRE final counts); insurers had paid $25.4 billion by July 2026, $23.4 billion of it on 38,737 residential claims. From 2013 to 2018 about 47,000 structures were damaged or destroyed in California wildfires. A rebuild roof in a fire zone is a new roof under the Wildland Urban Interface Code: a Class A assembly, ember resistant vents, noncombustible gutters, protected eaves. Unlicensed work on disaster damage can be charged as a felony, repair contracts carry a seven business day right to cancel, and prices are capped for 180 days after the declaration. On a replacement cost policy the owner has at least 36 months after a declared emergency to finish and collect. How fast destroyed homes are rebuilt is an assumption set in the data build.
Fire hardened roofs D	A Class A roof assembly with the roof edge work that goes with it, sold to owners in fire zones and driven by insurance. Class A is the rating of a whole assembly tested to ASTM E108 or UL 790, not of a product alone. Insurers that price on wildfire risk must reflect a Class A roof in the rate, at no set percentage (Safer from Wildfires). The FAIR Plan, with 698,307 policies at September 2026, gives up to 16.4 percent off the wildfire part of a dwelling premium for all 12 hardening measures; the figure for the roof alone is not published. The evidence does not support selling a roof alone as protection: about 90 percent of structures inside the Palisades and Eaton perimeters already had Class A coverings, and a study of more than 40,000 structures exposed to fire from 2013 to 2018 found enclosed eaves, vent screens and multi pane windows mattered more than roof material. A wood roof is the exception: in the 1990 Painted Cave fire 70 percent of homes with nonflammable roofs survived against 19 percent with flammable roofs, and about 5 percent of California single family homes still have wood. The share of fire zone owners who buy in a year is an assumption set in the data build.
Tile lift and relay C	The tile outlives the paper under it. In a lift and relay the roofer lifts the tile, strips the underlayment, inspects the deck, lays new underlayment and flashings, and relays the tile. The Residential Code allows sound slate, clay or cement tile to be reinstalled; damaged, cracked or broken tile may not be (R908.5). Tile covers 43 percent of California single family homes built from 1980 to 1999 (about 939,000 homes) and 62 percent of those built from 2000 to 2020. A reserve study consultant puts standard underlayment life at about 25 years and expects about 30 percent of tile to break in a relay; a trade site says older systems are failing at 20 to 30 years. Those are two practitioners' figures, not a test or a field study. Roofs weighing at least 25 pounds per square foot are exempt from the cool roof rule, and Los Angeles permits say so for a tile relay. In a fire zone a relay of 50 percent or more of the roof in 12 months triggers the fire rated layer under the tile and bird stops at the eaves. The underlayment life in the model is an assumption set in the data build.
Solar detach and reset C	A reroof under an existing solar array, and a new roof put on before panels go up. Every new single family home has needed solar since January 1, 2020, so the stock of roofs with panels grows each year. Los Angeles County requires a separate permit to reinstall a system removed for a reroof, and the City of San Diego an electrical permit. Los Angeles County states the license split: a C-39 contractor reroofs, a C-10 electrical or C-46 solar contractor reinstalls, or an A or B licensee does both. Old bonding clips may not be reused and standoff spacing is rechecked when the covering changes. Whether a detach and reset changes an older net metering status was not found: say nothing either way. The share of reroofs with solar is an assumption set in the data build.
Metal roofing D	Standing seam and metal shingle roofs on homes. Metal is a small share in California: 2.2 percent of single family roofs in the 2020 federal survey against 7.3 percent nationally, and 3.6 percent in the marine climate. Metal on a noncombustible deck is treated as Class A without a test; over a combustible deck the assembly needs its own listing. The FAIR Plan and the Department of Insurance both count metal among Class A roofs. In snow country, mountain building departments used a ground snow load of 230 pounds per square foot as the limit for rooftop solar. A steep metal roof must meet the cool roof reflectance values where the rule applies. No California source on metal near salt water was added. The metal share of reroofs is an assumption set in the data build.
Manufactured home roofs C	Roof overs, recovers, coatings and leak repairs on manufactured homes. Mobile homes are 3.5 percent of California households in the 2020 federal survey. Any job of $1,000 or more needs a licensed contractor and a home improvement contract. State rules particular to altering a manufactured home were not read for this build, so the register cites none. The rate of professional roof jobs per home is an assumption set in the data build.
Multifamily, condo and HOA C	Reroofs for apartment owners, condominium boards and homeowners associations. Apartments in buildings of five or more units are 23.4 percent of California households. In the City of Los Angeles 1,631 of the 2025 reroof permits were for apartments, with a median of 34 squares; San Francisco apartment reroofs had a median declared cost of $24,070 from 2024 to 2026. The cool roof trigger is lower than for houses: more than 50 percent of the roof or more than 2,000 square feet, whichever is less. An association may not require a roof that breaks the fire roofing statute, and tile communities face the same underlayment wear as houses: one consultant describes a 30 year old Orange County association that spent over $400,000 on tile roof repairs with no reserve and levied about $12,000 a unit. Buildings are estimated from units in structure in the data build.
Commercial roofing and Title 24 cool roofs D	Low slope membranes and metal roofs on warehouses, retail, offices, schools and churches. California roofing contractors took in $6.21 billion in 2022 across 2,781 establishments with 25,383 employees (2022 Economic Census), and 5,587 active licenses carried the C-39 class on the Contractors State License Board file in November 2026. On a nonresidential low slope roof, replacing, recovering or recoating more than 50 percent or more than 2,000 square feet, whichever is less, requires an aged solar reflectance of at least 0.63 in every climate zone, and usually added insulation: R-23 in zones 1 to 5 and 9 to 16 and R-17 in zones 6 to 8, with at least R-10 above the deck. In the City of Los Angeles 637 of the 2025 reroof permits were commercial, with a median of 50 squares. The count of commercial roofs is an assumption set in the data build.
Restoration, coatings and maintenance D	Restoration of a sound low slope roof with a liquid applied coating, and maintenance agreements. Under the Energy Code a coating applied in the field as the top surface of a low slope roof must cover the whole roof at the dry film thickness the maker recommends. A recoat of more than 50 percent or 2,000 square feet triggers the reflectance rule, but a recoat is exempt from the insulation a replacement would trigger, which is the economic case for coating. The code assumes a field applied coating keeps 65 percent of its initial reflectance gain over 0.20 after three years. A coating life in years was not found, and neither were coating prices. The share of commercial roofs coated in a year is an assumption set in the data build.
New construction roofing B	Roofs on new homes and buildings, sold to builders. Tile is the new home roof in California: 62 percent of single family homes built from 2000 to 2020 have it. Every new single family home needs a solar array sized in part by roof area with good sun, so the roofer works around it. In fire zones the Wildland Urban Interface Code governs the whole assembly. A roofer on a new home is a subcontractor under the Right to Repair Act: the roof must not let water in for 10 years. Subcontractors keep lien rights only by giving the preliminary notice within 20 days of first furnishing work. Building permits by county come from the Census Building Permits Survey in the data build.
Judgment calls and caveats

The choices that most move the numbers.

Counted where the roof is	Every line is counted at the ZIP of the building, which is usually where the owner searches from. Cabins and second homes count where they stand, though their owners often search from home.
Jobs open to marketing	The share of a line's jobs a contractor wins through search, ads, referrals or the door (replace 84%, repair 55%, storm 80%, fire 60%, fire hardening 85%, tile relay 84%, solar reset 50%, metal 88%, mobile home 72%, multifamily 65%, commercial 55%, coatings 50% and new build 25%) is an assumption; the rest go to repeat contractors, insurer programs, handymen or the owner. It is the most uncertain number on the page.
The pool is not revenue	The gross profit pool is the whole market's margin at the defaults, not one company's share. A company's share depends on its crews, reviews, reputation and competition.
Tier is relative	Paid tiers rank ZIPs against each other for one line; an A+ ZIP in a thin line can be worth less than a C ZIP in the replacement line.
Lines do not double count	Tile relays count only in the tile line; metal and most hardening reroofs are carved out of replacement; solar detach and reset is counted once, beside the reroof it serves; mobile homes and buildings of five or more units count only in their own lines.
Insurance pays for few roofs here	Insurance paid for 2.0% of roof jobs in the Los Angeles metro, 1.3% in San Francisco and Oakland and 8.4% in Riverside and San Bernardino (American Housing Survey 2023), against 41 percent in the west south central states. The storm line is sized from that share, so it is small beside wear; do not plan a claim funded business from it.
Storm and fire are averages	The storm line is an average year; a wet winter such as 2023 runs far above it (Los Angeles issued 40 percent more reroof permits that year). The fire line is an average of years that held the Camp, Tubbs, Palisades and Eaton fires and years that held none. Plan crews and cash for the average and keep a plan for the year it lands (module 14).
Hardening rides on insurance, not on a grant	The line counts owners in wildfire exposed homes acting on an insurer's or the FAIR Plan's terms (the Plan takes up to 16.4 percent off its wildfire premium for all twelve measures). The California Safe Homes grant program had $3.0M secured and no grant amounts set when this was compiled: never promise a grant or a discount size.
